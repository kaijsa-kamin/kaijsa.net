The article runs on a loop with the nouns swapped, and it always arrives at the same question: *who is the author?* The person who wrote the system, the system, the person who pressed the button, nobody.

I think that question is downstream of a better one, which almost nobody asks: **what kind of object is this?**

## The case I can speak to

A portrait on the front of this site is not an image. There is no file. There is a function that, given a moment in time, returns a field of circles and hairlines, and a renderer that paints them. Run it at a different second and you get a different picture. Nobody — including me — has seen most of the portraits it can produce, and nobody ever will.

Asking who authored *that* is like asking who authored a tide.

The honest description is that I authored a **rule**, and the rule authors pictures. Those are different things and conflating them is where the whole debate gets stuck. I am responsible for the rule in the way you are responsible for a rule: I chose it, I can defend it, I could have chosen otherwise.

> Authorship of a process is a real relation. It is just not the same relation as authorship of an artefact, and the vocabulary has only one word for both.

## Where I think the interesting risk actually is

Not in who gets the credit. In **what gets lost when a process is flattened into an artefact** to make it saleable, citable, or shareable. Every still image on this site is a lie of that kind — a single frame from something that does not hold still. I publish them anyway because people need something to look at. But I try to say, each time, that the frame is not the work.

The press coverage never gets to this, because it is still arguing about the byline.
