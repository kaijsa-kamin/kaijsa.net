I had been describing the sunflower piece wrongly, and this is the paper that corrected me.

The framing I had been using was that the golden angle is the *optimal* packing solution and that phyllotaxis has converged on it. That is roughly true and completely misleading, because it implies something in the plant is solving a packing problem.

Douady and Couder's experiment is better than that. Drops of ferrofluid falling at regular intervals into a dish, in a magnetic field, repelling one another — no biology at all — settle into the same spirals, with the same divergence angle, at the right deposition rate. The angle is not selected. It *falls out* of "each new element appears as far as possible from the existing ones, one at a time."

## What I changed

I had been seeding my sunflower by computing 137.508° and multiplying. That produces a correct picture by copying the answer.

Now I place each seed by repulsion from the ones already down and let the angle emerge. It comes out at 137.5° to within a rounding error, and the piece is not visibly different — but it is a different claim. One is a drawing *of* phyllotaxis. The other is phyllotaxis, running.

> A pattern you have hard-coded and a pattern you have caused are the same image and different objects.

The seam is visible if you look: in the emergent version the innermost dozen seeds are slightly irregular, because the rule needs a few elements before it stabilises. The hard-coded one is perfect from the first seed. I keep the irregularity now. It is the only evidence that anything happened.
